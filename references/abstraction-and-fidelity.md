# Abstraction and scientific fidelity

## Governing principle

A scientific figure is an argument, not a photograph. Its purpose is to make the scientific claim legible. The correct abstraction removes visual noise while preserving every relation needed to interpret the claim.

## Required abstraction record

Every nontrivial Scene should declare:

- `target_claim` — the one sentence the reader should understand;
- `essential_information` — geometry, topology, values, labels, or relations that must remain;
- `supporting_information` — useful context that should remain subordinate;
- `omitted_information` — detail deliberately removed;
- `risk_of_misinterpretation` — shortcuts that could imply false scale, causality, topology, concentration, occupancy, or direction.

## Abstraction levels

- **literal/validated** — follows supplied coordinates, dimensions, values, or topology;
- **structural** — preserves topology and relative spatial relations but not exact dimensions;
- **conceptual** — communicates mechanism or sequence only;
- **decorative** — does not support the claim and should normally be removed.

Never present a structural or conceptual drawing as quantitatively exact.

## Representation test

Use 3D only when it increases information gain. Ask:

1. Does depth represent a physical coordinate, orientation, enclosure, layer, or occlusion?
2. Would 2D preserve the same claim with less cognitive load?
3. Is the 3D view creating hidden/ambiguous relations?
4. Are labels, units, and topology still readable at final publication size?

## Common failures

- adding shadows, textures, and depth to every object;
- preserving every real-world detail instead of the relevant mechanism;
- omitting a scientifically critical relation because it is visually inconvenient;
- implying continuous data with a decorative gradient;
- implying precise scale through a schematic object;
- using perspective to make a 2D logical diagram harder to read.
